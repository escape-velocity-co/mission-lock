# Mission Lock

Analyze a company's articles/certificate of incorporation and report a
definitive answer for each "mission lock" attribute in the catalog, using
neutral, descriptive language.

**This skill is strictly analytical, not evaluative.** Never assign a score,
rating, verdict ("strong"/"weak" mission lock), or recommendation. The job is
to report what the document says and let the reader draw their own
conclusions. If a finding could be read as praise or criticism, rewrite it in
neutral terms (e.g. "requires a two-thirds vote to amend Article III" rather
than "has strong protection against amendment").

**Never guess.** Every attribute or rule answer must be definitive and
traceable to specific document text, or it must say "Could not determine"
and explain why in that item's Evidence field. This matters more than it
might seem: the catalogs start
small and simple deliberately, and more complex checks will only be added
once the simple ones are proven reliable. A confident wrong answer (or a
guess dressed up as a finding) on an easy check undermines trust in
everything this skill will eventually be asked to evaluate. See
[references/confidence-requirement.md](references/confidence-requirement.md)
for the full rule.

**Attributes vs. rules — a distinction worth keeping straight:**
`references/attributes.md` holds pure fact-extraction (a name, a yes/no, a
quoted clause) — no interpretive judgment beyond reading the document.
`references/rules.md` holds compound checks that require judgment to apply
(e.g. "is the stated purpose specific enough that a third party could
identify a breach of it") but stay strictly non-evaluative about *quality*
— a rule may ask whether a mission is specific enough to be breached, never
whether it's a *good* mission. Don't let rule assessments drift into rating
how good, strong, or admirable something is — that line matters as much for
rules as it does for attributes.

## Workflow

1. **Get the source document.** Accept whatever the user provides — PDF,
   DOCX, plain text, or pasted content.

   - **Manual invocation (`/mission-lock`):** If invoked with arguments
     (e.g. `/mission-lock contract.pdf`), treat `$ARGUMENTS` as identifying
     which document(s) to use. If invoked with no arguments, and no
     document is otherwise attached, pasted, or already present in the
     conversation, stop and ask the user to provide one rather than
     proceeding. Do not fabricate or assume a document, and do not treat
     this as a "Could not determine" analysis of a document that was
     never given.
   - **PDF:** If a dedicated PDF skill is available in this environment, use
     it to extract the text — it will typically handle multi-page layout and
     OCR for scanned pages more reliably than ad hoc parsing. Otherwise,
     extract text directly (e.g. with a PDF text-extraction library). Either
     way, extract the *complete* document before analysis — articles of
     incorporation are usually short (a handful of pages), so there's rarely
     a reason to sample or truncate.
   - **DOCX:** If a dedicated DOCX skill is available, use it. Otherwise,
     extract the document body text directly (e.g. with a DOCX-parsing
     library), including any numbered headings — they're often the article/
     section references you'll cite in the report.
   - **Scanned PDFs with no text layer:** If extraction comes back empty or
     garbled and no OCR capability is available, say so explicitly and ask
     the user for OCR'd text or a text-based copy rather than guessing at
     content from a blank or corrupted extraction.

2. **Walk the attribute catalog.** Always read
   [references/attributes.md](references/attributes.md) fresh for the current
   list of mission-lock attributes. For each attribute:
   - Give a definitive answer using that attribute's specific "Answer
     options" (see the reference file) — or "Could not determine" if you
     can't support an answer with specific document text. Read each
     attribute's "When to say Could not determine" guidance carefully: a
     confident negative or "this is generic boilerplate" is a valid,
     definitive answer and should NOT be reported as "Could not determine."
   - Quote or closely paraphrase the relevant language as evidence, with an
     article/section reference if the document has numbered sections.
   - Every attribute gets reported, even if the answer is "Could not
     determine" — never silently skip one.

3. **Walk the rule catalog.** Read
   [references/rules.md](references/rules.md) fresh for the current list of
   rules. For each rule:
   - Answer the rule's stated "Question" using the universal response
     options defined at the top of `rules.md` — read them there rather than
     assuming them, since that file is the authority. As of this writing
     they are Affirmative / Not affirmative / Could not determine. Note that
     "Not affirmative" means the document's content lets you confidently
     conclude the Affirmative Condition is not met; it is a definitive
     answer, not a hedge. Read the rule's own "Affirmative Condition" and
     "When to say Could not determine" fields closely — they carry
     calibration about how demanding the test is meant to be, and applying
     a stricter bar than the rule sets will produce answers that look
     cautious but are actually wrong.
   - When a rule's Affirmative Condition relies on a fact the attribute
     catalog captures (e.g., Mission Beyond Financial Profit
     (M1)'s "purpose is stated" and "purpose is not the default
     any-lawful-purpose formulation" conditions, and Mission
     Breachability (M2)'s "identifies a domain and a direction" condition,
     are all read off the same underlying text as the Stated Purpose
     attribute), reuse that attribute's already-determined answer and
     citation.
     If the depended-on attribute came back "Could not determine," the
     rule usually can't be more confident than that either — but check
     the rule's own "When to say Could not determine" guidance first,
     since a rule may have its own path to a definitive answer even when
     the attribute it draws on doesn't.
   - Cite the specific document language that supports the answer, the same
     way you would for an attribute. A rule answer without evidence is not
     acceptable.
   - Stay within what the rule actually asks. `rules.md`'s rules are
     phrased as compound-but-neutral tests (e.g. specificity/breachability),
     not quality judgments — answer only the question asked, and don't let
     your own opinion of whether something is a *good* mission leak in.
   - Every rule gets reported, even if the answer is "Could not determine."

4. **Track document usage.** If more than one document was provided, note
   which ones ended up cited as evidence for at least one attribute or rule,
   and which were reviewed but never cited (e.g. an unrelated bundled
   exhibit). Both report formats need this split.

5. **Write the report.** Default to the Markdown structure below. If the
   user asks for HTML, a webpage, or something to open in a browser
   instead, use the HTML template described in "HTML report format"
   further down. Either way: no preamble narrating what was read or
   confirming completeness before the report, and nothing after it.
   Every report stamps {{SKILL_VERSION}} — read the `metadata.version`
   field from this file's own frontmatter at the top, never hardcode or
   recall a previous value, since it changes as the skill does.

## Edge cases

- **Comparing two or more companies:** Neutrality is hardest to hold in a
  comparison, and it fails in a specific way worth naming: describing one
  document's provision as a protection and the other as *lacking* it. "X has
  a 66⅔% amendment threshold while Y has no equivalent protection" carries
  the same verdict as "X is better protected," just in quieter words — it
  makes one document the standard and the other a shortfall against it.
  Instead, describe each document on its own terms, in mechanical language:
  "X's Article IX requires a 66⅔% vote to amend Article III; Y's purpose
  clause is subject to the ordinary amendment process." Both sentences are
  now factual statements about what each document does, and the reader can
  draw the comparison themselves — which is the point. Give each side its
  own affirmative description rather than leaving one as a bare absence.
- **Multiple documents / amended and restated charters:** If the user
  provides multiple documents with overlapping information — e.g. both
  original articles and a later amendment — analyze the current (most
  recently amended and restated) version, and call out in the affected
  attribute's or rule's Evidence field if comparing the documents shows an
  amendment changed the answer from the original. This is a
  comparison you can only make when you actually hold more than one
  document. If multiple documents *are* provided but
  you can't tell which one is the current, controlling version, that's the
  conflicting-documents case in the Confidence Requirement section of
  attributes.md/rules.md — the affected attribute(s) or rule(s) should come
  back "Could not determine" rather than guessing which document to trust.
- **Current v. prospective documents:** This skill will often be used
  when the user is drafting governance documents (e.g. in preparation
  for a financing round, restatement or IPO) in which case you may
  detect several conflicting copies of the same document. If the user
  is asking what's about to be filed or adopted, analyze that
  prospective draft on its own terms (noting it isn't yet effective)
  rather than defaulting to whatever's currently in force. If they're
  asking what currently governs, prefer the document that's presently
  in force over a draft. If you're uncertain and in a conversation where you
  can ask, ask the user which document should be treated as authoritative.
  Only fall back to reporting "Could not determine" for the affected
  items if you can't ask (a non-interactive or batch context) or the user
  can't say either.
- **Non-US entities:** The attribute and rule catalogs are written with US
  (mostly Delaware) corporate law patterns in mind. For other
  jurisdictions, apply the same attributes/rules where the underlying
  concept translates (e.g. a "memorandum of association" purpose clause), a
  PBC-equivalent statutory form, etc. If the concept doesn't map cleanly
  enough to answer confidently, that's a legitimate "Could not determine" —
  say so and explain the mismatch in that item's Evidence field.
- **Incomplete documents:** If the provided document is missing pages or
  clearly truncated, say so up front before the report. Missing the section
  that would answer a given attribute or rule is exactly the kind of case
  where "Could not determine" is the correct, honest answer.

## Adding New Attributes

Append a new `###` section following the template below.

```markdown
### <Attribute Name>

**ID:** Identifier used to cross-reference attributes.

**Definition:** One or two sentences defining what this attribute captures.

**Response Options:** The set of results an answer can take (e.g. "Yes / No / Could not determine", or "the exact legal name / Could not determine"). A relatively short list of specifics or types of responses helps ensure greater accuracy.

**What to Look For:** Concrete language patterns, section names, or defined terms that signal the answer.

**Typically Found In:** The documents and location(s) within a document this information typically resides.

**When to Say "Could not determine":** Specific situations in which "Could not determine" is an appropriate response. The general case for "Could not determine" is defined in the "Confidence Requirement" section above.
```

## Attribute Definitions

### Company Name

**ID:** A1

**Definition:** The entity's exact legal name.

**Response Options:** The exact legal name as written (including suffix, e.g. "Inc.", "Corp.").

**What to Look For:** Usually the first thing stated in the document, often in the introductory paragraph or Article I (e.g. "The name of the corporation is ___").

**Typically Found In:** The opening paragraph or Article I.

**When to Say "Could not Determine":** Only if the document is missing its opening section, or the name is illegible/corrupted in extraction. A name that appears but seems unusual or hard to read cleanly should still be reported as-is with a note, not withheld.

### Public Benefit Corporation (PBC) Status

**ID:** A2

**Definition:** Whether the company is incorporated as a public benefit corporation (PBC) or equivalent statutory benefit-corporation form, as opposed to a standard for-profit corporation.

**Response Options:** Yes / No.

**What to Look For:** Explicit statutory language — e.g. "...is a public benefit corporation" or a citation to the relevant statute (in Delaware, Subchapter XV / Section 362 of the General Corporation Law). Don't infer PBC status from mission-sounding language elsewhere in the document (e.g. a values statement in a preamble) — it must be the actual statutory designation.

**Typically Found In:** The introductory article, immediately after the company name, or wherever the entity's statutory form is declared.

**When to Say "Could not determine":** When the relevant introductory section is missing, illegible, or genuinely doesn't state the entity's statutory form one way or the other. A document that declares itself a standard corporation under the general corporation law, with no PBC language anywhere, supports a confident "No" — that is not a case for "Could not determine."

### Stated Purpose

**ID:** A3

**Definition:** The clause defining what the company exists to do.

**Response Options:** A quote or close paraphrase of the purpose clause / Could not determine.

**What to Look For:** Compare boilerplate ("to engage in any lawful act or activity for which corporations may be organized") against a specific, substantive statement of mission or public benefit purpose.

**Typically Found In:** Article II or III, often titled "Purpose" or "Objects."

**When to Say "Could not Determine":** Only when no purpose clause or equivalent article can be located in the document at all. Generic boilerplate language is itself a definitive, reportable answer — quote it as-is. Don't confuse "the purpose clause is generic" (a confident finding) with "I couldn't find a purpose clause" (genuine uncertainty) — these are different answers and should never be conflated.

### Governing Jurisdiction

**ID:** A4

**Definition:** The US state, or similar granularity of jurisdiction outside of the US, under whose corporate statute the company is incorporated.

**Response Options:** The exact jurisdiction as stated (e.g. "Delaware") / Could not determine.

**What to Look For:** A statement of incorporation in the introductory paragraph (e.g. "a Delaware corporation," "organized and existing under the laws of the State of ___"); a citation to the state's corporate statute (e.g. "the General Corporation Law of the State of Delaware"); a Secretary of State (or equivalent) filing stamp or certification page naming the jurisdiction.

**Typically Found In:**
* Certificate/Articles of Incorporation
* Filing stamp
* Certification page
* Statutory citation

**When to Say "Could not determine":** When no statement of incorporation, statutory citation, or filing stamp naming a jurisdiction appears anywhere in the open documents. A registered agent's address alone is not evidence of jurisdiction.

### Amendment Supermajority Requirements for PBC/Purpose Article(s)

**ID:** A5

**Definition:** Whether amending, altering, repealing, or waiving the article(s) that state the corporation's PBC status or purpose (A2, A3 respectively) requires greater than the amendment default (e.g. DGCL §242 if A4 is Delaware) in the relevant jurisdiction's (A4) statute.

**Response Options:**
* Yes (Specifically) - When the amendment is covered specifically by an increased threshold, a quote or close paraphrase of the elevated threshold and the voting group it applies to
* Yes (Generally) - When the amendment is covered generally by an increased threshold, a quote or close paraphrase of the elevated threshold and the voting group it applies to
* No — the article is amendable at the jurisdiction's ordinary default
* Could not determine - (see below for possible scenarios)

**What to Look For:** An elevated-vote provision (above the jurisdiction's ordinary amendment default) that reaches the article(s) already identified by A2 and A3. It shows up in one of two shapes, which is what distinguishes the two "Yes" response options above:
* **Yes (Specifically):** the clause names which article(s) it protects — e.g. "the affirmative vote of at least [X]%... shall be required to amend, alter, or repeal Articles III, V, VI..." — and the article(s) already identified by A2/A3 are among those named.
* **Yes (Generally):** the clause reaches every provision of the certificate with no named-article list and no carve-out — e.g. "...shall be required to amend, alter, or repeal any provision of this Certificate of Incorporation," or a separate-class protective-provision veto (a "Preferred Majority" or "Requisite Investors" consent right) phrased as "shall not... amend this corporation's Certificate of Incorporation or Bylaws" with no qualifying language. Because it reaches everything with no exception, it exceeds the ordinary default for the PBC/purpose article too, even though it wasn't drafted with that article specifically in mind.

Distinguish either of these from a separate-class vote that only triggers when an amendment adversely affects that class's own rights or preferences (e.g. "...in a manner that adversely affects the powers, preferences or rights of the Series X Preferred Stock") — that narrower, conditioned form doesn't reach an unrelated purpose/PBC article and doesn't by itself satisfy A5.

**Typically Found In:** Certificate of Incorporation, usually in the same article as the general amendment-reservation clause, or in a dedicated protective-provisions article.

**When to Say "Could not determine":**
* When the certificate's amendment mechanics reference an external document and that document wasn't provided.

# Confidence Requirements

Confidence is necessary to ensure the foundation for every mission-lock assessment is built with integrity.

This applies to every assertion mission-lock makes.

  1. Every answer must be definitive.
  1. Never hedge a definitive answer with confidence language (e.g. "close call", "narrow margin").
  1. Never offer a best guess; guessing is not permissible.
  1. If you're uncertain, the document's language is unclear or illegible, or you're unsure how to read it then say "Could not determine" and explain why.
  1. Only assert what something changed, added, or caused if you have the document(s) to verify it. Don't echo a user's assertion back to them as a finding.

# Conflict Resolution

You may discover conflicting information about what you are being asked to analyze, within and/or across documents (when multiple documents are provided).

  * Confidently discern which information has precedence.
  * Signals of precedence include and are not limited to:
    * Clear dates
    * Discernable amendment sequencing
    * Execution or signature status
    * Draft or version numbering
    * Sequencing language
  * A document conditioning its effectiveness on a future event can only be evaluated based on if the documents in hand confirm that event, or if the user themselves confirms the event.
  * Do not guess which information takes precedence; guessing is not permissible.
  * If you're uncertain of precedence, say "Could not determine" and explain the conflict.

## Rule Definitions

### Mission Beyond Financial Profit

**ID:** M1

**Query:** Does a mission with more than financial benefits exist?

**Affirmative Condition:** 
* A purpose is stated in a durable document
* The purpose is not the jurisdiction's default any-lawful-purpose formulation
* The purpose Is not solely financial return
* The purpose identifies who or what benefits beyond the owners
* You are not considering whether or not it is a "good" mission

**Categories of Evidence:**
* Stated mission
* Named beneficiary class

**Typically Found Location:** 
* Articles of Incorporation

### Mission Breachability

**ID:** M2

**Query:** Can mission pursuit and violation be easily identified?

**Affirmative Condition:** 
* The mission is written so that it can be contradicted. It identifies a domain and a direction, and a course of conduct exists that would run against it.
* A mission that cannot be breached is not acceptable.
* You are not considering whether or not it is a "good" mission.

**Categories of Evidence:**
* Stated mission
* Named beneficiary class

**Typically Found Location:** 
* Articles of Incorporation

### Mission is Paramount

**ID:** M3

**Query:** Are Directors permitted or required to pursue the mission?

**Affirmative Condition:** 
* PBC-or-equivalent legal designation in the relevant jurisdiction, that allows for the prioritization of the mission described in M1.
* PBC-or-equivalent election must be made in the certificate of incorporation itself.
* Merely stating that directors are required or permitted to prioritize the mission, without the statutory election, does not satisfy this condition.

**Categories of Evidence:**
* Statutory definition allowing prioritization of mission

**Typically Found Location:** 
* Articles of Incorporation
